Three chapters of this series broke a system the same way: something that had to be fresh was used twice. The same mistake, three different algorithms.

## Stream cipher keystream

A stream cipher XORs a keystream into the plaintext. Reuse the keystream and the XOR of the two ciphertexts equals the XOR of the two plaintexts, with the key gone entirely. A one-bit statistic detects reuse: ordinary English XORed with English has its high bit clear in every byte. Crib dragging then recovers text from there.

## ElGamal

Encrypting with the same random k twice makes the first half of both ciphertexts identical, and dividing the second halves cancels the shared secret. Knowing one plaintext reveals the other.

## ECDSA

Two signatures with the same k share the same r. Subtracting the s values gives k, and then one modular inverse gives the private key. On P-256 that took no brute force, and the attacker then signed new data that verified. Sony's PlayStation 3 shipped exactly this flaw.

## The common pattern and the fixes

In each case a value that must be unpredictable and unique is reused, and the algebra turns two outputs into one equation with one unknown. The fixes are the same shape too: derive the value deterministically from the key and message so it cannot repeat (RFC 6979, Ed25519), use constructions that avoid caller-supplied nonces, or use a misuse-resistant mode. AES-GCM carries the same risk: reusing a nonce under one key leaks the XOR of plaintexts and the authentication key.

The practical rule is to treat randomness and counters as part of your threat model, and to prefer APIs that generate them for you.
