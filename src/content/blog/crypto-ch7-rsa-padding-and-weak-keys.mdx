RSA is simple enough to do by hand and dangerous enough that almost every shortcut in it has been exploited.

## RSA with p = 61 and q = 53

The textbook example works out as follows: n = 3233, phi(n) = 3120, pick e = 17, and the private exponent is d = 2753. The message 65 encrypts to 2790 and decrypts back to 65. The tests decrypt every one of the 3,233 possible messages, not just that one.

## Why padding is not optional

The lab implements OAEP from scratch and checks it against a reference library in both directions. It also demonstrates three ways unpadded "textbook" RSA breaks:

- **Deterministic:** the same message always gives the same ciphertext, so guesses can be confirmed.
- **Malleable:** multiplying two ciphertexts yields the encryption of the product of the plaintexts. The demo turns 6 and 7 into 42 without the key.
- **Small message, small exponent:** with e = 3 the ciphertext is just the plaintext cubed with no modular reduction, so an ordinary cube root recovers "attack at dawn".

OAEP adds randomness, a redundancy check and full-width padding. It also returns one uniform error for every failure, because telling padding errors apart leads to Manger's chosen-ciphertext attack.

## Why exponentiation needs squaring

With a 2,048-bit modulus, computing a power with e = 65537 by repeated multiplication takes about **908 ms**. Square-and-multiply takes **178 microseconds**, about 5,100 times faster. For a 2,048-bit private exponent, square-and-multiply takes about 35 ms; the naive loop would need roughly 2^2047 multiplications, on the order of 10^604 years.

## When two keys share a prime

Two RSA moduli that share a prime factor are both broken by a gcd, which is fast. The lab builds a toy fleet of 600 devices with a weak prime pool and finds the shared factors with both pairwise gcds and batch GCD, recovering private keys and decrypting an intercepted message.
